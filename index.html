Where it started

It started with a crash.

I was in middle school in March 2020, watching every stock I’d ever heard of fall by an amount that didn’t seem possible. What stuck with me wasn’t the fall. It was the recovery. Within months the whole thing had come back, and the people who had the nerve to buy when everyone else was selling made a lot of money. That was the first time I understood that panic has a price, and that someone is always on the other side of it.

Later I got pulled into the Korean economy. I watched individual companies lose enormous amounts of value while the indexes kept climbing. A market and the companies inside it are two different stories, and the interesting work is telling them apart.

Real estate is the other thread. I’m drawn to how something as abstract as a rate decision ends up in the price of a house on my street. I can’t walk past a property without wondering what would make it worth more.

Right now

What I’m working through this month.

I read the Financial Times most mornings. These are the threads I keep pulling on.

Reading

Chip War changed how I see the economy.

Chris Miller’s book made me realize microchips are essentially a commodity, but a commodity with a handful of choke points. One fab, one lithography company, one shipping lane can bottleneck the world. Now I read every trade and tech headline looking for the bottleneck.

Chris Miller, Chip War
Following

How a war in the Gulf reaches a household in Seattle.

The question I’m tracing right now is what the conflict with Iran does to the U.S. economy. Oil first, then shipping and insurance, then inflation, then rates, then everything rates touch. I’m trying to follow the chain instead of reacting to the headline.

Financial Times, daily
Building

The toolkit, from the ground up.

Excel and financial statement basics on my own time. Python through my intro computer science course. I know exactly where I am on the curve, and I’m not pretending otherwise. That’s the point of applying.

Excel · Financial statements · Python

How my brain runs

I don’t have the capital yet. I have the habit.

I catch myself doing it constantly. A restaurant that’s packed but slow. A store with the wrong hours. A tired building on a good block. What’s wrong here, what would it cost to fix, and what would it be worth after?

That’s the part of private equity that pulls me in: finding a company that’s worth more than it’s currently run for, and doing the work to close the gap. In ten years I want to be doing that for a living. Right now it’s just how I look at the world.

Whatever I learn this summer compounds through three more years of coursework, three more summers, and every conversation I have on a team or in a classroom.
