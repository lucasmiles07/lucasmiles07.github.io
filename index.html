Why finance

A lot of moving, then a trip to Wall Street.

I spent most of my childhood, about eleven years, in California, but I’ve also lived in China, England, New Jersey, Washington, and now Pennsylvania. California felt like home, and the moves at different points in my life put me in new communities and taught me how to be comfortable around people with different backgrounds.

My interest in finance became much more concrete during a field trip to Wall Street with my high school, The Hill School. We visited the New York Stock Exchange and met professionals working in different parts of the industry. At first I was drawn to the energy of the exchange, but what stayed with me most was the people. They had taken very different paths into finance, yet they shared an ability to understand complicated information, form a clear view, and communicate it with confidence.

Because I had lived in several different places and communities myself, I was especially interested in how they connected global events to individual companies and industries. The trip showed me that finance isn’t just numbers or stock prices. It involves understanding businesses, people, and how a decision made in one market can have effects across the world.

That experience encouraged me to study economics and accounting more seriously. Investment banking interests me because it combines analytical work with constant exposure to new companies, industries, and people. It’s a field where I could use the adaptability I’ve developed while building the judgment and technical skills to contribute to important business decisions.

Learning

What I’m learning right now.

I read the Financial Times most mornings. These are the things I keep coming back to.

Reading

Chip War changed how I think about the economy.

Chris Miller’s book made me realize how important microchips are to the economy. They’re essentially a commodity, but one with only a few choke points. A single factory, lithography company, or shipping lane can become a bottleneck for the whole world. Now I notice those bottlenecks in the news.

Chris Miller, Chip War
Following

What the conflict with Iran could mean for the U.S. economy.

I’m trying to understand how the conflict with Iran could affect the U.S. economy. Oil prices first, then shipping and insurance costs, then inflation, then interest rates, and then everything rates touch. I’m trying to follow the chain instead of just reacting to headlines.

Financial Times, daily
Studying

The basics.

Introductory economics and computer science this semester, with Python through the CS course. I don’t have a finance or modeling background yet. I want to learn how companies are valued and financed from the ground up, and a program built around that is what I’m looking for.

A habit

I don’t have the capital or the training yet. But I think about it a lot.

I catch myself doing it often. A restaurant that’s busy but slow. A store with the wrong hours. A tired building on a good block. I start wondering what’s wrong, what it would cost to fix, and what it would be worth afterward.

That’s the part of investing that interests me most: a business or property that could be worth more than it is today, and the work it takes to get there. I have a lot to learn before I can do that properly. For now, it’s just how I look at things.

Why a first-year? Being the youngest in the room is something I’m used to.

I ask the seniors how they handle the course load, which classes changed how they think, how they got their internships, and what they wish they had done at eighteen. Then I try to act on it. Looking for people who are ahead of me and asking how they got there is already a habit.

Four years of varsity lacrosse also made me coachable. A coach tells you what you’re doing wrong in front of the whole team, and you fix it on the next rep. In Scouting, older patrol leaders mentored me for years, and then I did the same for younger Scouts. I know what good mentorship looks like from both sides.

And a first-year has time. Whatever I learn this summer has three more years of school and three more summers to build on. I’d put it to use.

What I want to learn

Investment banking first, then private equity, with real estate as a long-term interest. Ten years from now I’d like to be someone who can look at an underperforming company or property, see the gap, and have the skills to help close it.

What I’ll do with the summer
