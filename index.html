Why finance

It became real on the floor of the Exchange.

I’d paid attention to markets since middle school, when I watched everything fall in March 2020 and then come all the way back. But before a Hill School field trip to Wall Street, I knew finance mostly as a respected, competitive career. I didn’t really understand the people, the energy, or the range of work behind it.

On that trip we visited the New York Stock Exchange and met professionals from different backgrounds and roles across the industry. Walking onto the floor made an impression on me, because I could see information, current events, and major business decisions all being processed in real time. I liked the pace. But the conversations were what stayed with me. The people we met were sharp and confident, and each had taken a different path in. They talked about companies and markets in a way that connected economics, strategy, and what was happening in the world.

That trip turned finance from something I had heard about into an environment I could picture myself in. It pushed me to take economics and accounting more seriously, and to start learning how companies are valued, financed, and advised through big decisions.

What drew me most was the combination: learning about different businesses, working alongside ambitious people, moving fast as a team, and contributing to decisions with real consequences. That’s why investment banking appeals to me as a starting point, and private equity as where I’d like to end up. I know the work is demanding. Seeing it firsthand made me want it more, not less.

Right now

What I’m working through this month.

I read the Financial Times most mornings. These are the threads I keep pulling on.

Reading

Chip War changed how I see the economy.

Chris Miller’s book made me realize microchips are essentially a commodity, but a commodity with a handful of choke points. One fab, one lithography company, one shipping lane can bottleneck the world. Now I read every trade and tech headline looking for the bottleneck.

Chris Miller, Chip War
Following

How a war in the Gulf reaches a household in Seattle.

The question I’m tracing right now is what the conflict with Iran does to the U.S. economy. Oil first, then shipping and insurance, then inflation, then rates, then everything rates touch. I’m trying to follow the chain instead of reacting to the headline.

Financial Times, daily
Studying

Introductory economics and computer science this semester, with Python through the CS course. I don’t have a modeling background yet, and I’m not going to pretend I do. I’m trying to learn how companies are valued and financed from the ground up, and a program built around that is what I’m looking for.

A habit

I don’t have capital or training yet. I do have a habit of noticing.

I catch myself doing it a lot. A restaurant that’s busy but slow. A store with the wrong hours. A tired building on a good block. I start wondering what’s wrong, what it would cost to fix, and what it would be worth after.

That’s the part of investing that pulls me in: a business or a property that could be worth more than it is today, and the work of closing that gap. I have a lot to learn before I can do that properly. Right now it’s just the way I look at things.

Why a first-year. Being the youngest in the room is familiar.

I’m the freshman on a college lacrosse team, which means I spend every day around people who are two and three years ahead of me. I don’t wait to be taught. I ask the seniors how they handle the course load, which classes changed how they think, how they landed their internships, and what they wish they had done at eighteen. Then I try to act on it. Seeking out people who are ahead of me and asking how they got there is already a habit.

Four years of varsity lacrosse also made me coachable in a way that transfers. A coach tells you what you’re doing wrong in front of the whole team and you fix it by the next rep. In Scouting I was mentored by older patrol leaders for years, and then spent years doing the same for the younger Scouts. I know what good mentorship looks like from both sides.

And a first-year has time. Whatever I learn this summer has three more years of coursework and three more summers to compound. I’d use it.

What I want to learn

How private equity and alternative investors actually evaluate a business. How to read a set of financials and find what’s wrong. How to build a model I trust, and how to defend a view when someone smarter pushes back.

What I bring

I show up early and I finish what I start. I’m comfortable being the least experienced person in the room. And I’ll make the most of every hour, because I’m not doing this to check a box.

Where I want this to go

Investment banking as a starting point, then private equity, with real estate alongside it. Ten years out I’d like to be someone who can look at an underperforming company or property, see the gap, and have the skills to help close it.

What I’ll do with the summer

Treat it like a season. Do the reading, ask the questions that show what I don’t know yet, and leave with real research and valuation skills instead of a line on a résumé.
